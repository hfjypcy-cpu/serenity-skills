## CPO laser chokepoint pattern

Pattern: AI networking architecture changes, downstream companies announce scale-up or CPO programs, then a narrow upstream laser or external-light-source supplier becomes a possible bottleneck.

What to learn:

- Do not stop at Marvell, Broadcom, Nvidia, or hyperscalers.
- Map optical engines, external light sources, laser arrays, pluggables, FAU, and inspection.
- Treat customer mapping carefully. Some links are public, some are one-hop inference.
- Revenue may lag evidence because qualification happens before volume production.

Useful questions:

- Which supplier's removal delays a customer's product roadmap?
- Is the supplier sole source, primary source, or just one of many?
- Does the company have enough capacity or a credible foundry partner?
- Is the market valuing it on old revenue instead of future ramp?

## Hidden gold rush bottleneck pattern

Pattern: a tiny commodity, material, component, or equipment niche sits at the intersection of several large demand shocks. The niche can look too small for major investors until AI, defense, space, or sovereign supply-chain demand all need it.

What to learn:

- Start from the small market that most investors ignore, then ask which large systems break without it.
- The re-rating usually comes from strategic scarcity, not generic TAM expansion.
- A niche can be investable when supply is hard to expand, customer qualification is slow, and large buyers have limited substitutes.
- The hardest part is verifying that the company can capture the scarcity without being diluted or bypassed.

## Architecture keychain pattern

Pattern: a new AI or compute architecture has several "keys" that must all work before the downstream system can scale. The best research starts by naming each key, then finding the least-covered public company attached to it.

Examples of keys:

- Photonics materials and substrates.
- Silicon photonics and SOI wafers.
- CPO laser sources and external light sources.
- Epiwafers, InP, MBE tools, and specialty foundry capacity.
- Inspection, metrology, and yield tools that make production reliable.

What to learn:

- The downstream winner can be obvious while the key supplier remains ignored.
- A report that lists only large customers and module vendors can still be useful because its omissions point toward hidden suppliers.
- The edge improves when several independent key maps point to the same small company.
- Each key needs a timing check. Some keys matter now, while others remain several years away.

## Customer-mapped single-stock pattern

Pattern: a single stock becomes interesting when its hidden customer map, production partners, and future product timing make it more important than current revenue suggests.

What to learn:

- Separate confirmed customers from high-confidence inference and open hypotheses.
- Use archived webpages, product specs, funding amounts, geography, unit volumes, and competitor filings to triangulate.
- A customer map is useful only if the revenue opportunity is material relative to the company.
- The thesis should include what would prove it wrong: failed qualification, dual sourcing, capacity delay, or financing stress.

## Functional monopoly pattern

Pattern: a small company controls a process, patent, material, or tool that appears minor until a new architecture needs it at scale.

Examples of roles:

- CPO inspection and yield.
- HBM4 compression or metrology.
- Glass-core-substrate LIDE/TGV equipment.
- Silicon photonics substrates.
- InP substrate chain.
- MBE or hybrid-level deposition systems.

What to learn:

- The moat may be process know-how, patent control, certification, or capacity allocation.
- The market may not understand the distinction between adjacent technical categories.
- The best clue is often management language about market share, yield, or inability to license around the process.

## HBM4 metrology pattern

Pattern: HBM generation shift creates new bonding, overlay, inspection, and metrology needs. Domestic champions may benefit when Samsung, SK Hynix, or Micron want local alternatives to US or Japanese tool monopolies.

What to learn:

- Start from the specific HBM architecture change, not the generic memory cycle.
- Identify whether the tool is needed for HBM4, HBM4E, or HBM5.
- Separate first customer qualification from broad adoption.
- Watch whether a supplier is used only by one memory maker or across the industry.

## Glass substrate equipment pattern

Pattern: AI accelerators and high-end packaging push substrate constraints. If glass substrates enter mass production, the first winners may be tools, photoresists, etch, dicing, bonding, and inspection suppliers.

What to learn:

- Tool suppliers can benefit before material suppliers if capacity buildout starts early.
- "First to supply" and "in discussions with multiple companies" are useful but not enough. Check production dates and scale.
- Estimate whether tool count and ASP matter relative to market cap.

## Foundry chokepoint pattern

Pattern: the key supplier may not be the branded module or component company. It may be the specialty foundry or manufacturing partner that controls high-yield capacity for several downstream photonics or RF customers.

What to learn:

- Map who fabricates the hard part, not only who sells the final product.
- A foundry can become a second-order beneficiary when many customers need the same process window.
- Customer concentration, capacity allocation, and capex timing matter more than broad market excitement.
- Check whether the foundry's relevant segment is large enough to matter to earnings.

## NeoCloud capex funnel pattern

Pattern: hyperscaler AI demand exceeds internal capacity, so capex flows into third-party compute providers or miners pivoting to HPC. The small company can grow much faster than the hyperscaler because the starting base is tiny.

What to learn:

- Contract quality matters more than press-release excitement.
- Financing structure matters because GPU and power buildouts are capital intensive.
- Compare secured capacity, contracted customers, gross margin, and dilution.
- Avoid treating all "AI data center" names equally.

## Space and Starlink supplier pattern

Pattern: a large satellite or defense architecture can create narrow demand for RF, optical, power, material, and ground-infrastructure suppliers that are not obvious from the prime contractor.

What to learn:

- Start from deployment cadence, funded contracts, and hardware architecture.
- Look for components that must be qualified and produced repeatedly.
- Defense and space revenue often has long timing, export-control, and customer-concentration risk.
- Treat promotional small caps carefully when financing depends on repeated capital raises.

## Stablecoin payment-friction pattern

Pattern: a regulated stablecoin issuer can monetize reserve assets and reduce payments friction. The thesis depends on regulation, distribution, reserve yield, and banking competition.

What to learn:

- Stablecoin issuers may be better understood as financial infrastructure than ordinary fintech.
- Interest rates and reserve mix affect earnings.
- Legislation can create opportunity or hand control to incumbents.
- Payment friction examples are useful, but they must connect to revenue model.

## Too-early technology pattern

Pattern: a technology is real but still too far from volume ramp. Early investors may finance R&D and dilution while waiting.

Examples:

- Quantum dots or some MicroLED paths before clear production timing.
- Plastic optical cables if volume is several years out.
- Robotics components without credible production volume.
- Exotic thermal materials for a 2028+ bottleneck.

What to learn:

- Public market timing often works best when the ramp is close enough for investors to front-run revenue.
- Record the trigger that would change the decision: customer qualification, revenue guide, capacity expansion, or standards adoption.
